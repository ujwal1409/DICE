The coordination process

From requirement to delivery

Requirement, technical review, partner match, execution and delivery — one consistent route for every enquiry, whatever the capability mix turns out to be.

The five steps

Select a step to see what happens

The same route runs from first contact to handover. Choose any step below to read what goes in, what comes out, and who is responsible for it.

Step 1

Requirement

The customer submits a drawing, scope, sketch, or problem statement. There is no need to arrive with a finished specification — a description of the problem is a valid starting point, and often the more useful one.

What helps most

  • Drawings, sketches or photographs
  • Scope or problem statement
  • Quantities and target timeline
  • Applicable standards, if known

What comes back

  • Acknowledgement of the enquiry
  • A single named point of contact
  • Any immediate clarifying questions
Step 2

Technical Review

Clarify specifications, constraints, materials, quantities, and execution requirements. This is where ambiguity gets removed before it becomes an expensive problem downstream, and where the realistic process route starts to emerge.

What gets examined

  • Materials, grades and condition
  • Tolerances and surface finish
  • Applicable codes and standards
  • Access, handling and site constraints

What comes back

  • A clarified, written scope
  • Risks and open questions flagged
  • A proposed process route
Step 3

Partner Match

Identify suitable specialist capabilities for the requirement. Selection follows the clarified scope rather than a fixed supplier list, and the arrangement is confirmed for the specific project before anything starts.

What drives selection

  • Process and equipment required
  • Material and size envelope
  • Inspection and qualification needs
  • Availability against the programme

What comes back

  • The capability route for the work
  • Interfaces and responsibilities defined
  • Indicative commercial basis
Step 4

Execution

Coordinate engineering, manufacturing, fabrication, or other agreed activities. DICE keeps hold of the customer conversation while the work runs, so progress, changes and problems surface through one channel.

What is in place first

  • Approved scope and released drawings
  • Agreed inspection and test points
  • Programme and sequence

What comes back

  • Progress updates against the programme
  • Inspection records as work proceeds
  • Early notice of any change or delay
Step 5

Delivery

Close out the work against the agreed requirement. Delivery is not just dispatch — it is the point where what was built is checked back against what was asked for, with the documentation to show it.

What is checked

  • Completed work against the agreed scope
  • Outstanding items and concessions
  • Documentation completeness

What comes back

  • Dispatch or site handover
  • Documentation pack for the scope
  • Formal close-out against the requirement

This describes the coordination process DICE follows. It is not a contractual commitment, and the documentation, inspection and commercial arrangements for any particular scope are confirmed per project.

Engineering workflow

Input → Interpret → Model → Validate → Release

Inside step two and step four, engineering work follows its own sequence. Each stage has a defined purpose, and nothing is released until it has been checked.

Stage 01

Input

Receive and log everything the customer provides — drawings, sketches, samples, specifications and revision history — so there is one agreed starting point.

Stage 02

Interpret

Establish the actual design intent, identify constraints, and list what is missing. Assumptions are written down rather than left implicit.

Stage 03

Model

Build the 2D or 3D representation and develop it into manufacturing documentation that a workshop can work to without interpretation.

Stage 04

Validate

Check the output against specification, applicable standards and buildability. Where analysis is in scope, load cases and assumptions are agreed first.

Stage 05

Release

Issue controlled drawings with clear revision status, so everyone downstream is working from the same version of the truth.

Why it is sequenced this way

Interpretation before modelling, validation before release

Most rework in fabrication traces back to a drawing that was modelled before the intent was properly understood, or released before it was checked. Keeping interpretation and validation as explicit stages — rather than things that happen informally — is what makes the downstream route predictable.

  • Assumptions recorded, not carried in someone's head
  • Revision status visible on every issued document
  • Buildability considered while changes are still cheap
Engineering & CAD capability

Same process, different mix

The capability mix changes. The coordination process does not.

Three illustrative requirements, three different partner mixes — and in every case the same five steps, the same single interface, and the same technical review before anything is routed.

Drawing to fabricated assembly

A customer supplies a general arrangement and needs a welded assembly delivered to specification with inspection records.

Capability mix

  • Engineering & CAD
  • Fabrication
  • Welding
  • NDE

Constant: requirement, technical review, partner match, execution, delivery.

Machined component batch

A repeat batch of close-tolerance components, where material and finish decide which machining route is appropriate.

Capability mix

  • Engineering & CAD
  • CNC Machining
  • Dimensional Inspection

Constant: requirement, technical review, partner match, execution, delivery.

Site installation with trades

Work that is mostly people rather than product — qualified trades supporting installation and fit-up on a live site.

Capability mix

  • Specialist Execution
  • Manpower
  • Site Coordination

Constant: requirement, technical review, partner match, execution, delivery.

These three scenarios are illustrative examples used to show how the capability mix varies. They are not descriptions of completed DICE projects, and the capability names refer to the categories listed on the services page, not to named partners.
