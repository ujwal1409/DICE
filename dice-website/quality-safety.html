Quality & Safety

Safety, Quality, Delivery, Teamwork and Innovation — the principles applied to every coordinated scope, and the requirements that should be agreed before any work starts.

How DICE approaches it

Principles are only useful if they change decisions

These five principles are not decoration. They describe how an enquiry is reviewed, how a capability is selected, what gets agreed before execution, and what is checked at handover.

The five DICE principles

Safety

Safe working practice is treated as a precondition of execution, not a box ticked afterwards. If a method cannot be carried out safely, it is the wrong method.

  • Unsafe working environments are not accepted
  • Access, handling and permit constraints raised at review stage
  • Site activity planned around the conditions actually present

Quality

Quality requirements have to be defined before they can be met. What "acceptable" means for a product or a service is established up front, in writing, for both parties.

  • Acceptance criteria agreed, not assumed
  • Work checked against drawing and specification
  • Inspection points identified before execution begins

Delivery

Delivery is measured against the agreed customer requirement and scope — not against a convenient internal interpretation of it.

  • Scope and delivery expectations agreed in writing
  • Realistic commitments rather than optimistic ones
  • Early notice when something changes

Teamwork

A coordinated model only works if the coordination is real. DICE, the customer and any engaged specialist capability work to one shared scope definition rather than three separate readings of the same drawing.

  • Interfaces between DICE and specialist partners defined before work starts
  • One scope definition carried into every downstream activity
  • Problems surfaced through a single channel, not between suppliers
  • Responsibility for the customer conversation stays with DICE
How the partner network is coordinated

Innovation

Practical improvement rather than novelty for its own sake. The useful kind of innovation is usually a better process route, a simpler joint, or a design change that makes something buildable.

  • Design intent improved where it helps manufacture
  • Alternative process routes offered when they suit the requirement better
  • Buildability considered while changes are still inexpensive
  • Solutions aimed at the customer's actual problem

Before anything is made

Quality Requirements Should Be Defined Before Execution.

Most quality disputes are not really disagreements about workmanship. They are disagreements about what was required in the first place — discovered after the work is finished, when it is expensive to resolve.

The practical answer is unglamorous: write the requirements down before execution and agree them with everyone who will be held to them. Depending on the project, that may include any of the following.

  • Quality plans What will be controlled, by whom, and at which points in the sequence.
  • Inspection requirements Which inspections apply, who witnesses them, and what is recorded.
  • Non-destructive examination (NDE) scope Which methods, applied to which joints, and to what extent — confirmed per project rather than assumed.
  • Documentation Material traceability, weld records, test reports and the handover pack expected at completion.
  • Acceptance criteria The measurable basis on which the work will be judged complete and acceptable.

Define it, agree it, then execute it — in that order.

What this page does not claim

This page describes working principles and the requirements that should be agreed before execution. It deliberately makes no claims about the following:

  • No certifications or accreditations are claimed for DICE or for any partner.
  • No compliance with any specific named standard is asserted. Where a standard applies to a scope, it is identified and agreed for that project.
  • No quality management system registration, audit outcome or third-party approval is stated.
  • No performance statistics, defect rates or on-time delivery figures are published.

Any certification, approval or standards compliance relevant to your project should be confirmed in writing and independently verified before you rely on it.
